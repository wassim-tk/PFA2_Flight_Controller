Step 1Math

Choose the state

Every simulation starts with the same question: which numbers do you need to know right now to predict the next instant? Those numbers are the state. For a quadcopter you need where it is, how fast it moves, how it is oriented, how fast it rotates, and how fast each propeller spins.

\[ x = \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{p} \\ \mathbf{v} \\ \boldsymbol{\eta} \\ \boldsymbol{\omega} \\ \boldsymbol{\Omega} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{16},\quad \begin{aligned} &\mathbf{p} = [x,\,y,\,z] &&\text{position, world frame (m)}\\ &\mathbf{v} = \dot{\mathbf{p}} &&\text{velocity, world frame (m/s)}\\ &\boldsymbol{\eta} = [\phi,\,\theta,\,\psi] &&\text{roll, pitch, yaw (rad)}\\ &\boldsymbol{\omega} = [p,\,q,\,r] &&\text{body angular rates (rad/s)}\\ &\boldsymbol{\Omega} = [\Omega_1 \dots \Omega_4] &&\text{motor speeds (rad/s)} \end{aligned} \]

The whole model then reduces to one line: find a function \(f\) such that \(\dot x = f(x, u)\), where \(u\) is what you command (the four motor speeds). Once you have \(f\), MATLAB or Simulink can integrate it forward in time. Steps 2 and 3 build \(f\) piece by piece.

Conventions used in your code: body frame x forward, y left, z up; Euler angles in ZYX order (yaw, then pitch, then roll). Writing these down first saves hours of sign errors later.

blocks/state_out.m

y   = x(1:12);     % [pos; vel; eul; omega]  -> sensors / controller
pos = x(1:3);
eul = x(7:9);
w   = x(13:16);    % motor speeds
Step 2Math

Motors and the allocation matrix

One propeller

A propeller spinning at \(\Omega_i\) pushes up with a force and resists rotation with a drag torque. Both grow with the square of speed. The motor and ESC cannot change speed instantly, so they are modelled as a first-order lag with time constant \(\tau_m\).

Math
\[ F_i = k_f\,\Omega_i^2,\qquad Q_i = k_m\,\Omega_i^2 \]\[ \dot\Omega_i = \frac{\Omega_{\text{cmd},i} - \Omega_i}{\tau_m} \]
MATLAB · quad_dynamics.m
w_c = min(max(w_cmd, 0), P.w_max);
dw  = (w_c - w) / P.tau_m;
F   = P.kf * w.^2;

\(k_f\) and \(k_m\) come from a thrust-stand test: measure thrust and torque at several speeds and fit a parabola. \(\tau_m\) is the time for the speed to reach 63% of a step command.

Four propellers: thrust and torques

The body does not care about individual forces. It responds to their total: one collective thrust \(T\) and three torques \(\tau_x, \tau_y, \tau_z\). A force \(F_i\) at position \((x_i, y_i)\) pointing up produces the torque \(\mathbf{r}_i \times \mathbf{F}_i = (y_i F_i,\; -x_i F_i,\; 0)\). The yaw torque comes from propeller drag: a counter-clockwise propeller pushes the body clockwise, so \(\tau_z = -\frac{k_m}{k_f} s_i F_i\) with \(s_i = +1\) for CCW.

With arm length \(L\) and \(d = L/\sqrt2\), the motors sit at 1 FR \((d,-d)\), 2 RL \((-d,d)\), 3 FL \((d,d)\), 4 RR \((-d,-d)\). Collecting everything gives a 4×4 matrix:

\[ \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} T \\ \tau_x \\ \tau_y \\ \tau_z \end{bmatrix}}_{u} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1 \\ -d & d & d & -d \\ -d & d & -d & d \\ -c & -c & c & c \end{bmatrix}}_{A} \begin{bmatrix} F_1 \\ F_2 \\ F_3 \\ F_4 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad c = \frac{k_m}{k_f} \]

This matrix is used twice. The plant uses \(A\) to turn motor forces into thrust and torques. The mixer uses \(A^{-1}\) to do the reverse: given the thrust and torques the controller wants, find the four motor forces.

quad_params.m

P.motor_xy  = [ d -d;  -d  d;   d  d;  -d -d];   % [x y] of motors 1..4
P.motor_dir = [ 1  1  -1  -1];                   % +1 = CCW seen from above
P.A = [ ones(1,4);
        P.motor_xy(:,2)';          % roll  torque =  y_i * F_i
       -P.motor_xy(:,1)';          % pitch torque = -x_i * F_i
       -c*P.motor_dir ];           % yaw   torque from prop drag
P.Ainv = inv(P.A);

At hover the thrust equals the weight, so each motor carries a quarter of it: \(\Omega_h = \sqrt{mg/(4k_f)} = \sqrt{1.2 \cdot 9.81 / (4 \cdot 8.5\times10^{-6})} \approx 588\) rad/s, about 5 600 rpm. Keep that number in mind; it shows up in your plots.

Step 3Math

Rigid-body equations

Rotation matrix

Thrust always points along the drone's own z axis. To know where that is in the world, you need the rotation matrix \(R\) from body to world. For ZYX Euler angles it is \(R = R_z(\psi)\,R_y(\theta)\,R_x(\phi)\):

\[ R=\begin{bmatrix} c_\psi c_\theta & c_\psi s_\theta s_\phi - s_\psi c_\phi & c_\psi s_\theta c_\phi + s_\psi s_\phi\\ s_\psi c_\theta & s_\psi s_\theta s_\phi + c_\psi c_\phi & s_\psi s_\theta c_\phi - c_\psi s_\phi\\ -s_\theta & c_\theta s_\phi & c_\theta c_\phi \end{bmatrix} \]

The last column of \(R\) is the body z axis seen from the world. That column is the direction the thrust pushes.

Translation (Newton)

Gravity pulls down, the thrust pushes along the body z axis, and air drag slows the drone in proportion to its speed:

Math
\[ \dot{\mathbf p} = \mathbf v \]\[ m\dot{\mathbf v} = \begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\-mg\end{bmatrix} + R\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\T\end{bmatrix} - k_d\,\mathbf v \]
MATLAB · quad_dynamics.m
dpos = vel;
acc  = [0; 0; -P.g] ...
     + R*[0; 0; T]/P.m ...
     - (P.kd/P.m)*vel;

Rotation (Euler's equation)

Torques change the angular rate. The term \(\boldsymbol\omega \times J\boldsymbol\omega\) is the gyroscopic coupling between axes; it is small for a quad but costs nothing to include. The Euler angles then change according to the body rates through the matrix \(W(\boldsymbol\eta)\):

Math
\[ J\dot{\boldsymbol\omega} = \boldsymbol\tau - \boldsymbol\omega \times J\boldsymbol\omega \]\[ \dot{\boldsymbol\eta} = \underbrace{\begin{bmatrix} 1 & s_\phi t_\theta & c_\phi t_\theta\\ 0 & c_\phi & -s_\phi\\ 0 & s_\phi/c_\theta & c_\phi/c_\theta \end{bmatrix}}_{W(\boldsymbol\eta)}\boldsymbol\omega \]
MATLAB · quad_dynamics.m
Wm = [1, sph*sth/cth, cph*sth/cth;
      0, cph,         -sph;
      0, sph/cth,     cph/cth];
deul   = Wm * omega;
domega = P.J \ (tau - cross(omega, P.J*omega));
Why the 25° tilt limit matters. \(W\) divides by \(\cos\theta\). At θ = ±90° it becomes infinite (gimbal lock). Your controller never lets the drone tilt past 25°, so Euler angles are safe here. Aerobatic models use quaternions instead.

Ground contact

Without a ground, the drone would fall forever before take-off. The model adds a simple rule: if the drone is at z ≤ 0 and is being pushed down, freeze its position and angles and damp its velocities to zero. As soon as the thrust exceeds the weight, the rule stops applying and it lifts off.

Step 4MATLAB

The plant as a MATLAB function

Steps 1 to 3 together are \(\dot x = f(x, u)\). In MATLAB this becomes a single function that takes the state and the input and returns the derivative. It does not integrate anything; it only answers the question "how fast is each state changing right now?".

blocks/quad_dynamics.m (structure)

function dx = quad_dynamics(x, w_cmd, P)
    % unpack the state
    vel = x(4:6); eul = x(7:9); omega = x(10:12); w = x(13:16);
    % motors            -> dw, F
    % allocation        -> u = P.A * F  -> T, tau
    % rotation matrix   -> R
    % Newton            -> dpos, acc
    % Euler             -> deul, domega
    % ground contact    -> override if on the ground
    dx = [dpos; acc; deul; domega; dw];
end

Before plugging this into anything, test it on its own. A good first check is hover: at 1 m altitude, with every motor already at \(\Omega_h\), every derivative should be zero.

quad_params; addpath blocks
x  = [0;0;1; zeros(9,1); P.w_hover*ones(4,1)];
dx = quad_dynamics(x, P.w_hover*ones(4,1), P);
disp(norm(dx))         % should print ~1e-15

You can then integrate it with ode45 directly in MATLAB, without Simulink. Command a bit more speed on motor 3 (front left) and the drone should roll right and drift to the right (−y):

w = P.w_hover*[1 1 1.01 1]';            % motor 3 slightly faster
[t, X] = ode45(@(t,x) quad_dynamics(x, w, P), [0 2], x);
plot(t, rad2deg(X(:,7)))                % roll angle grows positive
                                        % positive roll = right side down,
                                        % thrust tilts toward -y (right)

This "open-loop poke" is the fastest way to catch sign errors in the allocation matrix. Do it for each axis before writing any controller.

Step 5MathMATLAB

Linearize at hover to design gains

The full model is nonlinear, but near hover (small angles, ψ = 0, \(T \approx mg\)) it simplifies to a set of independent double integrators. Use \(\sin\alpha \approx \alpha\), \(\cos\alpha \approx 1\), and drop the small products:

\[ \ddot x \approx g\,\theta,\qquad \ddot y \approx -g\,\phi,\qquad m\ddot z \approx T - mg \]\[ J_{xx}\ddot\phi \approx \tau_x,\qquad J_{yy}\ddot\theta \approx \tau_y,\qquad J_{zz}\ddot\psi \approx \tau_z \]

Read these as cause and effect: tilt forward (θ) and you accelerate forward; roll right (φ > 0) and you accelerate toward −y; push more thrust and you climb. linearize_hover.m computes the same thing numerically, as the full 12×12 \(A\) and 12×4 \(B\) matrices, by perturbing each state.

PD on a double integrator

Every channel above has the form \(M\ddot e = \text{command}\). A PD law, command \(= K_p e + K_d \dot e\), turns it into a mass-spring-damper:

\[ M s^2 + K_d s + K_p = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \omega_n = \sqrt{\frac{K_p}{M}},\qquad \zeta = \frac{K_d}{2\sqrt{K_p M}} \]\[ \boxed{K_p = M\,\omega_n^2,\qquad K_d = 2\,\zeta\,\omega_n\,M} \]

So you do not guess gains. You pick how fast (\(\omega_n\)) and how damped (\(\zeta\)) each loop should be, then compute them. This is exactly how your altitude and position gains were set:

Loops inside loops: time-scale separation

The position loop does not command torques directly. It asks for a tilt angle, the angle loop asks for a body rate, and the rate loop produces the torque. This only works if each inner loop is several times faster than the one around it, so the outer loop can treat it as instantaneous. A factor of 3 to 5 is the usual rule:

LayerBandwidthWhere it comes from
Motors33 rad/s1/τm = 1/0.03
Rate (roll, pitch)20 rad/sKp,rate/Jxx = 0.15/0.0075
Angle (roll, pitch)6 rad/sKp,att = 6
Position1 rad/sωn chosen above

Tuning order on a real drone follows the same chain from the inside out: rate first, then angle, then altitude, then position.

Step 6MathMATLAB

The cascaded controller

Position → tilt angles

The PD law gives a desired horizontal acceleration \(\mathbf a_d\). The drone gets horizontal acceleration only by tilting its thrust, so invert \(\ddot x \approx g\theta\), \(\ddot y \approx -g\phi\). First rotate \(\mathbf a_d\) into the yaw frame, because "forward" for the drone changes when it yaws:

Math
\[ \mathbf a_d = K_p(\mathbf p_{ref} - \mathbf p) - K_d \mathbf v \]\[ \begin{bmatrix}a_x^b\\a_y^b\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}c_\psi & s_\psi\\ -s_\psi & c_\psi\end{bmatrix}\mathbf a_d \]\[ \theta_d = \operatorname{atan2}(a_x^b, g),\quad \phi_d = \operatorname{atan2}(-a_y^b\cos\theta_d, g) \]
MATLAB · controller.m
e_p = ref(1:2) - pos(1:2);
a_d = C.pos.Kp(:).*e_p - C.pos.Kd(:).*vel(1:2);
a_d = min(max(a_d, -C.acc_max), C.acc_max);
ax_b =  cos(psi)*a_d(1) + sin(psi)*a_d(2);
ay_b = -sin(psi)*a_d(1) + cos(psi)*a_d(2);
theta_d = atan2(ax_b, P.g);
phi_d   = atan2(-ay_b*cos(theta_d), P.g);

Altitude → thrust

Start from the weight \(mg\) (the feed-forward that holds hover), add a PID correction, then divide by \(\cos\phi\cos\theta\): when tilted, only that fraction of the thrust points up.

Math
\[ T = \frac{mg + K_p e_z + K_d(\dot z_{ref} - \dot z) + K_i\!\int\! e_z}{\cos\phi\cos\theta} \]
MATLAB · controller.m
iz = iz + C.alt.Ki*ez*Ts;      % clamped
T  = P.m*P.g + C.alt.Kp*ez ...
   + C.alt.Kd*(vz_ref - vel(3)) + iz;
T  = T / max(cos(eul(1))*cos(eul(2)), 0.5);

Angle → rate → torque

The angle loop is a plain P controller producing a rate setpoint. The rate loop is a PID that outputs torque. Two practical details matter here: the derivative acts on the measured rate rather than the error (so a setpoint step does not cause a spike), and it is low-pass filtered because differentiating a noisy gyro amplifies the noise.

Math
\[ \boldsymbol\omega_{sp} = K_{att}(\boldsymbol\eta_{sp} - \boldsymbol\eta) \]\[ \boldsymbol\tau = K_p \mathbf e_\omega + K_i\!\int\!\mathbf e_\omega - K_d\,\tfrac{d}{dt}\boldsymbol\omega\big|_{\text{filtered}} \]
MATLAB · controller.m
rate_sp = C.att.Kp(:).*e_att;
e_r   = rate_sp - omega;
d_raw = (omega - omega_prev)/Ts;
dfilt = dfilt + alpha*(d_raw - dfilt);
tau = C.rate.Kp(:).*e_r + irate ...
    - C.rate.Kd(:).*dfilt;

From continuous math to discrete code

A flight controller runs in a loop every \(T_s\) = 4 ms. Integrals and derivatives become sums and differences, and the controller's memory is kept in persistent variables:

ContinuousDiscrete (each tick)
\(\int e\,dt\)i = i + e*Ts, then clamp to ±Imax (anti-windup)
\(\dot\omega\)(omega - omega_prev)/Ts
Low-pass at fcd = d + alpha*(d_raw - d), with \(\alpha = T_s / (T_s + 1/(2\pi f_c))\)

Mixer

Finally, invert the allocation matrix, clip negative forces (a propeller cannot pull down), and convert force to speed with \(\Omega = \sqrt{F/k_f}\).

blocks/mixer.m

F = P.Ainv * u;          % [T; tau] -> 4 forces
F = max(F, 0);
w_cmd = min(sqrt(F / P.kf), P.w_max);
Step 7Simulink

Wiring it in Simulink

Simulink adds the two things your MATLAB functions lack: time and memory. The central idea is a single feedback loop. Your plant function computes \(\dot x\); an Integrator block integrates it into \(x\); that \(x\) is fed back into the plant. The solver (ode45) chooses the time steps.

The ideas that make it work

  • Plant = continuous, controller = discrete. The physics runs on the continuous solver. The two Zero-Order Hold blocks sample the reference and the measurements every 4 ms, so the controller sees what a real microcontroller would see and only updates at 250 Hz.
  • MATLAB Function blocks. Each blocks/*.m file is pasted into a MATLAB Function block. Its inputs and outputs become the block's ports automatically.
  • Parameters as a struct. P lives in the base workspace. Inside each block it is declared with scope Parameter (not Input), so it does not need a wire.
  • Persistent variables = controller memory. Integrators and the previous gyro value survive from one 4 ms tick to the next.
  • Logging. To Workspace blocks store signals in out.pos, out.eul and so on, which plot_results reads.

Building it by hand in the Simulink editor

  1. Run quad_params so P exists. Create a blank model and set the solver to ode45, stop time P.Tsim, max step 1e-3 (Modeling → Model Settings → Solver).
  2. Add a MATLAB Function block (User-Defined Functions), double-click it, and paste the contents of quad_dynamics.m.
  3. In that editor open Edit Data (or the Symbols pane), select P and change its scope to Parameter. Its input port disappears.
  4. Add an Integrator (Continuous) with initial condition P.x0. Wire Plant output → Integrator → back to the Plant's x input.
  5. Repeat step 2–3 for mixer, controller, reference, state_out, sensors.
  6. Add the two Zero-Order Hold blocks with sample time P.Ts, a Clock, a Random Number block for noise, scopes and To Workspace blocks. Wire as in the diagram.
  7. Run. If a port size error appears, it usually means a function returns a row where a column was expected.

What build_quad_model.m does instead

The script performs exactly those clicks through the Simulink API, so the model can be rebuilt from text files at any time:

new_system('quad_sim'); open_system('quad_sim');
add_block('simulink/User-Defined Functions/MATLAB Function', 'quad_sim/Plant');
ch = sfroot().find('-isa','Stateflow.EMChart','Path','quad_sim/Plant');
ch.Script = fileread('blocks/quad_dynamics.m');        % paste the code
pd = ch.find('-isa','Stateflow.Data','Name','P');
pd.Scope = 'Parameter';                                % P is a parameter
add_block('simulink/Continuous/Integrator', 'quad_sim/Integrator', ...
          'InitialCondition', 'P.x0');
add_line('quad_sim', 'Plant/1', 'Integrator/1');       % wire dx -> 1/s
add_line('quad_sim', 'Integrator/1', 'Plant/1');       % wire x  -> plant
save_system('quad_sim');
Editing workflow. If you change blocks/*.m, rerun build_quad_model because run_sim only builds when quad_sim.slx is missing. If you change quad_params.m, run it again before simulating, because the model only loads it when P does not already exist.

Checking the results against the math

A simulation is only trustworthy if its numbers match what the equations predict. Your run from earlier confirms the model on every point that can be calculated by hand:

QuantityPredictedSeen in your plots
Hover motor speed\(\sqrt{mg/4k_f}\) = 588 rad/s ≈ 5 615 rpm≈ 5 600 rpm
Hover thrustmg = 11.8 N≈ 11.8 N
Pitch peak for the 2 m x stepatan(Kp·2 / g) = atan(2/9.81) = 11.5°≈ 11.5°
Roll sign for a +y movenegative (ÿ ≈ −gφ)≈ −11.5°
x settling time (2%)≈ 4/(ζωn) = 4.4 s, plus inner-loop lag≈ 5–6 s
Yaw slewlimited to 60°/s → 90° in ≥ 1.5 sstraight ramp, ≈ 1.8 s
Altitude overshoot for a stepζ = 0.9 → \(e^{-\pi\zeta/\sqrt{1-\zeta^2}}\) ≈ 0.2%12% at end of take-off

The last row is the interesting one. A PD with ζ = 0.9 should barely overshoot, so the 12% is not caused by the gains. It comes from what the linear analysis leaves out: the climb-rate feed-forward drops from 0.75 m/s to zero in one tick when the ramp ends, and the altitude integrator charged up during the climb. This is how the math helps you debug. When the simulation disagrees with the prediction, the gap points to the part of the model or controller the prediction ignored.

Step 9MATLABSimulink

Build it yourself in four exercises

Reading the full model is one thing; rebuilding a small version of it is what makes it stick. Each exercise adds one idea.

Exercise 1 · Altitude only, pure MATLAB

One axis, \(m\ddot z = T - mg\), with the PD gains from Step 5. State is \([z,\ \dot z]\).

m = 1.2; g = 9.81; Kp = 4.8; Kd = 4.3; zref = 1.5;
T = @(x) max(m*g + Kp*(zref - x(1)) - Kd*x(2), 0);   % controller
f = @(t,x) [x(2); T(x)/m - g];                       % plant: xdot = f(x,u)
[t, x] = ode45(f, [0 10], [0; 0]);
plot(t, x(:,1)); grid on; xlabel('t [s]'); ylabel('z [m]')

Change ζ by editing Kd and compare the overshoot with the formula.

Exercise 2 · Same thing in Simulink with basic blocks

  1. Blocks: Step (final value 1.5), Sum (+−), Gain Kp, Gain Kd, Constant m·g, Sum (+++), Gain 1/m, Constant g, Sum (+−), two Integrator blocks, a Scope.
  2. Chain: thrust → 1/m → minus g → Integrator (gives ż) → Integrator (gives z) → Scope.
  3. Feed z back to the first Sum (error), ż to the Kd gain, and add Kp·e − Kd·ż + m·g to make the thrust.
  4. The plot should match Exercise 1 exactly. If it does, you understand how ode45 and the Integrator block relate.

Exercise 3 · Replace the plant with a MATLAB Function block

Delete the 1/m, −g and first integrator, and put a MATLAB Function block in their place returning dx = [x(2); T/m - g], followed by one Integrator with a 2-element initial condition [0;0]. This is the exact pattern your full model uses, just smaller.

Exercise 4 · One rotation axis with a cascade

Roll only: \(J_{xx}\ddot\phi = \tau_x\). Add an angle P loop (gain 6) feeding a rate P loop (gain 0.15) and watch how the response changes when you lower the rate gain until it is no longer faster than the angle loop. That is the time-scale separation rule from Step 5, seen directly.

After these four, the full model is the same pieces repeated in three dimensions: Exercise 3 for the plant, Exercise 4 for each rotation axis, Exercise 1 for altitude, and one more PD layer on top for position.

Equations, gains and code excerpts are taken from the files in drone_simulink/ (quad_params.m, blocks/*.m, build_quad_model.m). The measured values in Step 8 are read from the simulation plots of the 32 s mission.
